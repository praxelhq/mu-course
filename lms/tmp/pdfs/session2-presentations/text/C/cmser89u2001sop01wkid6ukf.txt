TrustMRR 1,000 — What the
Numbers Are Really Saying
This consultant read walks the dataset scene by scene. Across 1,000 businesses the story
repeats: a handful of winners dominate revenue and growth while the majority are tiny,
flat, or inactive. Below we move through eight scenes that reveal how averages mislead,
who’s for sale, how prices behave, and what categories and countries actually mean.
Scene 1 — Concentration at
the Top
The dataset of 1,000 businesses is highly concentrated. The top 10 businesses capture
48% of every dollar of monthly recurring revenue (MRR). Expanding to the top 50
accounts for 71% of MRR. One business — Stan — alone earns more than the bottom
several hundred combined. This is not a marketplace of many similar firms but a few
breakout winners sitting atop a long thin tail.
Scene 2 — The Typical Business Is Tiny
Remove the giants and the median business earns only $282/month in recurring revenue. Nearly 1 in 5 businesses (197) report $0 recurring revenue, many
with no revenue in the last 30 days. This dataset reads like a builder ecosystem—idea-stage, side projects, or pre-traction ventures—so modest gains (a few
hundred dollars/month) can already be ahead of most peers.

health — it describes a mirage created by a few spikes, not the common experience.

Scene 4 — Who Is Selling?
262 businesses are listed for sale. The for-sale group has roughly one-sixth the typical
monthly revenue ($187 vs. $1,055) and less than one-quarter the growth rate (10% vs.
42%) compared to non-listed businesses. That indicates owners are generally selling
stalled or weak businesses, not cashing out winners — listings are more a symptom of
stagnation than a healthy exit market.
Scene 5 — Pricing Multiples Vary Wildly
Asking prices correlate with revenue overall, but revenue multiples swing from under one year to over 300x monthly revenue. The most extreme multiples
belong to listings earning almost nothing, where even a small price produces an enormous, misleading multiple. Therefore a single multiple is not a reliable
shortcut — examine absolute revenue first before trusting multiples.

Scene 6 — B2B vs. B2C
Trade-offs
B2B businesses earn about 5.7× more in typical monthly revenue than B2C ($1,036 vs.
$181). Yet B2C is more numerous (401 vs. 255) and grows faster on average. The trade-off:
B2B captures more revenue per customer (fewer, bigger checks, slower), while B2C
requires scale (many smaller, faster-moving customers). Choosing which to build or buy is
a decision about customer type and monetization strategy.
Scene 7 — Geography: Volume vs. Monetization
The U.S. accounts for the largest share of total revenue and its typical business earns the most. France ranks second by count but its typical business earns
a fraction of the U.S. typical. India has even more businesses than France, yet its typical business earns nearly nothing. Volume does not equal monetization
— lots of listings from a country can hide weak revenue performance.

Scene 8 — AI Is Common, But
Not Universally Winning
AI is the most common category and generates the most combined revenue, yet the
typical AI business earns only $446/month — modest compared to categories like
Education or Analytics. The AI category’s aggregate strength is carried by a few large
winners above many small, unproven products. “AI” as a label is not a substitute for
evidence of strong monetization.
The Bottom Line — How to
Read This Market
Across every cut of the file the shape repeats: a handful of standout businesses hold most
revenue and growth while the majority are small, flat, or being sold off. Over half of total
revenue is behind listings with no category, and 129 anonymized “Stealth” businesses
hold more than a quarter of revenue. Recommendations: don’t benchmark against
averages (they’re distorted by outliers); use the median as the honest baseline; treat “for
sale” as a warning flag; and view category or country breakdowns as partial until
uncategorized revenue is identified. The dataset lacks answers for why 129 companies are
anonymized or what drove extreme outliers — those require external investigation.
