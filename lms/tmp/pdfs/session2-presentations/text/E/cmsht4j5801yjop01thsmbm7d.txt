What to Build Next
Everything in this report is read off TrustMRR.
TrustMRR is a public database of startups whose revenue is verified: founders connect their Stripe,
RevenueCat or Paddle account and their monthly number goes on record, refreshed hourly. Many of the
same businesses are simultaneously listed for sale with an asking price.


That makes it the closest thing there is to an open order book for small software businesses — and the basis
for everything that follows: what is being solved, what is growing, and where the openings are.

       How to read it
       Five ways to mine the site. The leaderboard is the least useful page on it.



   1       Read the for-sale listings, not the rankings.

           Sellers disclose revenue, costs, margin and why they are leaving. It is a free teardown of a business that already works, written by the person who ran it.


   2       Hunt for the third company doing the same thing.

           One is a fluke. Two is a coincidence. Three separate people making money the same way is demand you can trust without asking anyone.


   3       When the winners in a category share one channel, that channel is the moat.

           The barrier to entry is not the product. It is whatever it took them to own that channel — and that is what you'd have to beat.


   4       A high-revenue business listed for sale is usually a service wearing software clothing.

           Before copying the model, work out what a human has to do every month. Either avoid it or make automating that the whole product.


   5       Shared tech stacks across the top names mean speed is table stakes.

           If everyone can ship the same thing in a week, shipping fast is not an advantage. Assume the build takes days and the distribution takes a year.

      Build for the buyer from week one.

      Documented, no manual monthly steps, no dependency on you personally. That is the whole difference between an asking price of one times and two and a half.

      Treat anything under one times as a warning.

      Cheap multiples usually mean a human is doing the work behind the product. Find out what that human does before you copy the business.

      Consider running the trade in reverse.

      At these prices, buying a working $500–2,000 a month product and pointing your distribution at it beats starting from zero.

What to do with that
  1       Pick one channel and hold it for eighteen months.
          Three channels for six weeks each is the most common way a working product quietly dies.

  2       Expect the unglamorous ones to win.
          Search, written content, Reddit, cold email, affiliates and integrations are what the listed businesses actually run on.

  3       Treat being quoted by AI assistants as a second, separate job.
          Most incumbents are still optimising only for the ten blue links. That gap closes fast — it is open now.
